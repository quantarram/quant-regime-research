Hit rate here counts a win when the target is hit first, or when the 21 days end with a positive return. The break-even hit rate is |average loss| ÷ (average win + |average loss|), the stock version of 1 ÷ odds. It sits well below the hit rate for any long position because most bets end on the time limit with small gains; the fair comparison is the “same ETFs always-long” column, i.e. what buying every one of these ETFs on every day would have scored.
How this works: the same recipe as the football board. A pick appears only when both form conditions hold on the latest close: 1-month return in the bottom 10% of the ETF's own last 5 years and 1-month volatility relative to 1-year volatility in the top 10% — percentile cut-offs of each ETF's own point-in-time history, not fitted values. Of the 99 two-condition and one-condition rules tested, the rule is chosen each year using only outcomes already complete before 1 January, and the table above pools the following year's bets, never seen when the rule was chosen. A rule may fire on at most 6% of ETF-days; a horizon publishes picks only if its pooled out-of-sample cluster t-statistic is at least 2.

Read this before using it: the effective sample is the number of separate market episodes, not the number of bets — ETFs dip together, so 8,751 bets stand on roughly 127 months, and the t-statistic is indicative only. The edge is in equity-type ETFs; bond and commodity ETFs show none (table above), and 2026 so far is negative. Single stocks are excluded: the ten in the stored price panel were picked after they became large, which inflates any backtest. Costs are 5 bps round trip; entry is assumed at the next close after the signal, so there is no look-ahead, but real fills will differ. Validated on past data only; the live record above is the real test.
